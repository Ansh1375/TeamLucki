**OVERVIEW:**
The goal of this project is to examine the relationship between socioeconomic conditions and crime patterns in Chicago by combining crime data from the City of Chicago Open Data Portal with socioeconomic data from the United States Census Bureau. More specifically, we want to see whether crime rate is associated with variables such as income or education level across different areas of the city. By integrating these two datasets, we hope to better understand how crime patterns vary across neighborhoods and whether broader social and economic conditions may help explain some of those differences. To complete this project, we will first collect and document both datasets, including their structure, source information, and any legal or ethical considerations. Next, we will assess data quality by checking for missing values, duplicates, inconsistencies, and possible issues with geographic identifiers. After that, we will clean both datasets by standardizing column names, formatting values, and selecting the variables that are most relevant to our question. We will then integrate the datasets in Python using shared identifiers such as community area, ZIP code, or police district. Once the merged dataset is created, we will analyze crime frequency and crime type patterns alongside socioeconomic indicators and create visualizations to show the results. Finally, we will document the workflow carefully so that the full process is reproducible and easy to follow.

**DATASETS:**
This project will examine the relationship between socioeconomic conditions and crime patterns in Chicago by integrating two complementary datasets from the City of Chicago and the United States Census Bureau. The first dataset, obtained from the City of Chicago Open Data Portal, contains detailed records of reported crimes in the city. These records include information such as crime type, date and time, location, and geographic identifiers like community area. This dataset makes it possible to analyze how crime frequency and crime types vary across different parts of Chicago. The second dataset, obtained from the United States Census Bureau, contains demographic and socioeconomic information for geographic areas within Chicago. Relevant variables include median household income, education levels, employment status, poverty rates, and population characteristics. These datasets can be linked using shared geographic identifiers such as police district, ZIP code, and community area. After aggregating crime incidents by neighborhood or other geographic units, the crime data can be matched with the socioeconomic indicators for the same areas. This integration will allow us to evaluate whether differences in socioeconomic conditions are associated with differences in crime patterns across Chicago neighborhoods.

**CONSTRAINTS:**
There are many considerations that need to be made in terms of using this data. One consideration is the completeness and accuracy of the data. Many crimes go unreported, and these data likely understate the total number of crimes committed in Chicago. Another consideration is the ethics behind this analysis. Analyzing crime data alongside socioeconomic indicators can raise ethical concerns if the results are interpreted in ways that stigmatize specific communities. We must make sure that we are interpreting the data correctly and understand the outside factors that might influence this data. Another consideration is matching data between the two datasets. For example, socioeconomic data is typically collected through surveys done over multiple-year periods. However, crime data is updated as it occurs. Because of this difference, it might be difficult to line up the data exactly. 

**GAPS:**
There are also some gaps in comparing these two datasets. One gap is the missing external factors. The crime dataset from the City of Chicago Open Data Portal and the socioeconomic data from the United States Census Bureau primarily focus on crime incidents and demographic or economic characteristics. However, many other factors can influence crime rates, such as policing strategies, housing conditions, access to public services, or neighborhood infrastructure. Without data on these additional variables, the analysis may not fully capture all factors affecting crime patterns. Another gap is the limited information on specific individuals. Both datasets provide aggregated information rather than detailed individual-level data. The census data reports statistics for geographic areas rather than specific households, and the crime dataset describes incidents rather than the broader social conditions of individuals involved. This limits the ability to analyze more detailed relationships between socioeconomic characteristics and crime.
